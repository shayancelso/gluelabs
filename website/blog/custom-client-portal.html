How to build a custom client portal: options, cost, timeline

By the Gloo team · 14 August 2026 · 5 min read

There are three ways to get a client portal: switch on the one hiding inside software you already own, rent a portal platform for $50–$500 a month, or build your own for $15,000–$40,000 in three to six weeks. Which one is right depends on a single question: is the portal a filing cabinet, or is it how clients experience your firm?

This guide covers the four features clients actually use, the three routes with honest trade-offs, and the security lines you cannot cross when other people's documents are involved.

What a client portal actually is

Strip away the vendor language and a client portal is four features. A firm — accountants, lawyers, agencies, contractors, clinics — gives each client a login, and behind it the client can:

1. Upload documents — and, crucially, see a checklist of what you still need from them. The checklist is the feature: it is what ends the "just resending in case" email chain.

2. See status — where their work stands, without calling to ask. Half the inbound "quick question" emails a service firm receives are this.

3. Message you in context — a thread attached to their file, replacing email attachments and the compliance nightmare of documents in inboxes.

4. See their history — invoices, past deliverables, last year's return, in one place, forever.

Everything else vendors demo — e-signatures, scheduling, payment collection — is sometimes useful and easily added later. Firms that try to launch with ten features ship none well. The four above account for nearly all real usage.

Check what you own first. Practice-management and project tools often include a basic portal, and if all you need is the filing cabinet, free wins. This is the advice a portal vendor won't give you; it is also the honest starting point.

Rent a platform when your workflow is standard and you can accept working inside someone else's shape and sending clients to someone else's domain.

Build when the portal is the client experience: your process modelled properly (not approximated by someone's configuration options), your brand on your domain, integration with the systems you already run, and no per-client fee quietly compounding as you grow. A firm with 200 clients paying $3 per client per month is spending $7,200 a year renting something it could own.

The security non-negotiables

A portal holds other people's tax documents, contracts, and financial records. Whichever route you take, four things are not optional:

Data separation enforced in the database. Client A must be structurally unable to see client B's data — row-level security on every query, not links that are merely hard to guess or buttons that are merely hidden. This is the check most cheaply-built portals fail, and it is the difference between an embarrassing bug and a reportable breach.

Private file storage with expiring links. Documents live in a private bucket and are served through short-lived signed URLs. A document URL that works in an incognito window is a breach in waiting.

Encryption in transit and at rest. Table stakes, but ask anyway.

An audit trail. Who uploaded, viewed, downloaded, and when. The first time a client disputes what they sent you, this pays for itself.

If you started one in an AI builder

Plenty of firms have half a portal built in Lovable or Bolt — and the visible half is often genuinely good. The four items above are exactly the invisible half AI builders skip. Before real client documents touch it, run it against our production-readiness checklist.

What a build looks like

Week 1: map the actual workflow — what clients send, what they ask, what staff re-type where. The portal's spec is your inbox's greatest hits.

Weeks 2–4: build the four features against your real process, wire in the systems you already use, and load a handful of pilot clients.

Weeks 4–6: pilot feedback, hardening pass on the security items above, then invite clients in batches. The measure of success is boring and wonderful: the "where is my file?" emails stop.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a custom client portal cost?

In 2026, a custom client portal — secure logins, document upload, status tracking, messaging — typically costs $15,000–$40,000 and takes three to six weeks. Portal-feature add-ons to software you already own cost little or nothing; off-the-shelf portal platforms run $50–$500 a month but force clients into their workflow and branding.

Should I buy a client portal platform or build a custom one?

Check the software you already use first — many practice-management and project tools include a basic portal, and free is hard to beat. Buy a portal platform if your workflow is standard and you can accept its shape. Build custom when the portal is part of how clients experience your firm: your branding, your process, your data model, and no per-client fees as you grow.

What features should a client portal have?

Four things account for nearly all real usage: secure login, document upload with a clear checklist of what is still missing, status visibility (where is my work), and a message thread that replaces email attachments. Most other features go unused. Build the four well before adding anything.

How do I keep client data secure in a portal?

Non-negotiables: every query filtered by the authenticated client (row-level security, enforced in the database, not just hidden in the interface), files stored privately with expiring signed links, encryption in transit and at rest, and an audit trail of who accessed what. If a vendor cannot explain how client A is prevented from ever seeing client B’s data, walk away.
