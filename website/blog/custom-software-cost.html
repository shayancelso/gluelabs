Pricing

How much does custom software cost in 2026? Real numbers

By the Gloo team · 14 August 2026 · 5 min read

In 2026, a focused internal tool costs $8,000–$20,000. A client portal or workflow app with logins and permissions costs $15,000–$40,000. A customer-facing product costs $25,000–$75,000. Timelines are weeks, not months.

If you have collected quotes and they span a 10× range for the same brief, nothing is wrong with your brief. The bidders are pricing different delivery models. This post explains the bands, what moves a project inside them, and how to tell an honest quote from an expensive one. Or skip the reading and use the calculator.

Why every quote you got is different

A business owner recently told us their friend paid over $12,000 for a custom app to run a car rental company, and asked whether that was reasonable. The honest answer: it depends entirely on what the $12,000 bought, and the fact that nobody can tell from the price alone is exactly the problem with this market.

Custom software has no sticker price because vendors price their cost structure, not your problem. A traditional agency carries a project manager, a designer, two developers, and an account lead across a three-to-six-month engagement. They cannot quote $15,000 and survive. A freelancer with no process can quote $3,000 and disappear in week four. A small senior team using AI-assisted development can genuinely deliver in two to three weeks what took a 2019 agency a quarter — and price accordingly.

Same brief, three cost structures, three honest-but-incompatible quotes.

What actually moves the number

Within a band, four things drive most of the variance — and they are worth knowing because they are the levers you control:

Integrations. Every system the tool must talk to — CRM, accounting, a legacy database — adds $2,000–$6,000 and real risk. Cutting an integration in v1 (import a CSV for the first month instead) is the single cheapest scope decision you can make.

Permissions. "Everyone on my team sees everything" is nearly free. "Admins, managers, and staff see different things" adds a few thousand. "External clients log in and must never see each other's data" is a security boundary that deserves engineering, and costs like one.

Data migration. Moving years of spreadsheet history in cleanly is $1,500–$4,000. Skipping it and starting fresh is free — and often the right call.

AI features. Summaries, drafting, scoring, chat over your data: $4,000–$12,000 depending on depth. Worth it when it removes a human bottleneck, decorative otherwise.

The number that matters more than price

Ask what happens in month four when the team wants a new field and an upstream API changes. A build with no maintenance answer is not cheaper — it is deferred cost. Every project should end with either a named internal owner or a modest monthly arrangement with the builder.

What AI changed — and what it didn't

AI-assisted development is why the bands above start at four figures, not six. The first working version of most business tools now takes days. We have published a commit-by-commit build log of a real 150-store tool reaching production in two days if you want the receipts.

What AI did not change: the production layer. Authentication that can't be bypassed, permissions that hold, backups, payments that reconcile, an app that survives its hundredth concurrent user — this is still engineering, and it is where DIY builds on tools like Lovable or Bolt tend to stall at "90% done". If you have one of those, the production-readiness checklist will tell you where you stand. The economics of AI builders versus hiring are covered in a separate post.

How not to get burned

Four questions separate honest quotes from expensive ones, whoever you talk to (including us):

1. "What do I own at the end?" The only acceptable answer is: the code, the database, and the accounts they live in. If the vendor keeps the code, you are renting, not buying.

2. "What does the money buy in people and weeks?" This one question explains any 10× quote spread in minutes.

3. "What is explicitly out of scope?" A fixed price with no scope document is a future argument with an invoice attached.

4. "What happens after launch?" See the callout above. No answer, no deal.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a custom app cost for a small business?

In 2026: an internal dashboard or reporting tool runs $8,000–$20,000, a client portal or workflow app with logins and permissions runs $15,000–$40,000, and a customer-facing product runs $25,000–$75,000. Timelines are weeks, not months. Quotes far above these bands are usually pricing in the vendor’s overhead, not your problem.

Why did one agency quote $12,000 and another $90,000 for the same app?

They are pricing different delivery models, not different software. A traditional agency carries project managers, designers, and a bench of developers across a three-to-six-month engagement. A small senior team using AI-assisted development ships the same tool in weeks. Ask each bidder what the money buys in people and weeks, and the difference explains itself.

Is it cheaper to build the app myself with AI tools?

Cheapest in cash, most expensive in time and risk. AI builders like Lovable get a non-technical founder to a working demo for under $100 a month, and that is genuinely the right way to validate an idea. Where they stall is the production layer — authentication, permissions, data separation, payments. Budget for professional hardening if real customers or client data will touch it.

What ongoing costs should I budget after the build?

Hosting is usually $20–$100 a month at small-business scale. The real ongoing item is maintenance: someone must fix breakages when an API changes and add the fields the team asks for. Budget a modest monthly maintenance arrangement or name an internal owner — unowned tools die in month three.
